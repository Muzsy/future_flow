## 1. Enough to Take the Next Step

In November, you begin with a few evenings and the space between blocks of text. Your office work continues; digital design is still an experiment, something you can approach without asking it to support you.

Planning helps you put the sample content in order. Writing helps you decide what needs to be said. Neither quite tells you how much space a heading should have, or why a page can feel crowded even after you have shortened its sentences. You adjust the layout, pause, and look again. The changes are small enough to feel almost invisible, yet making them teaches you to notice. Basic digital layout becomes something you can practice rather than merely imagine.

By January, you give the experiment a boundary: one fictional website brief. The long page of text offers a problem smaller than changing your whole career. You turn it into a simple wireframe, arranging what comes first and what can wait farther down the page. There is relief in a task with edges. You can finish this exercise without knowing how an independent working life would fit together.

When it is done, the distinction matters: you have completed a practice brief. You have not discovered whether anyone will pay you to do the same.

In March, you write a short case study about your choices. Here, your reasoning comes more readily than your sense of completion. You can explain why you grouped content or gave one section more emphasis; deciding that the design needs no further adjustment is harder. Still, the explanation gives the work another kind of clarity. You now have an introductory case study alongside the layout and wireframing practice—not a replacement income, but a starting point you can examine.

By early April, the next question is no longer simply whether you can begin. You have begun. The question is what your limited evenings should be for.

Your office job still brings in 400,000 HUF a month, and your liquid funds remain 1,200,000 HUF. Neither next step requires spending those savings now. Both keep your job and your base in Veszprém, close to the life you want your creative work to fit around.

You outline a small local test: prepare one narrowly scoped design offer and seek interest near home, using up to four of your eight available weekly hours. That would bring the question of demand closer, but leave less time for polishing—and secure neither a client nor income.

The other path gives those four hours to further practice, aiming for two additional fictional case studies. It offers a quieter learning pace, while postponing the question your exercises cannot answer.

Both possibilities remain open. Which deserves your evening time now: testing a small independent offer locally, or building a stronger portfolio first?

## 2. The Edges of an Offer

In May, you bring your ambition down to the size of a single page: a website layout, using text supplied by the client, with one revision. Written plainly, the offer looks almost too small to hold your wish for more creative work. Yet it is something you can explain—and something you can fit alongside your office job.

The harder work lies around its edges. You spend more thought on what is not included than on what to call the service. Each boundary gives the offer shape. Within your planned four evening hours a week, you learn to define a small design task without letting it expand into an open-ended obligation. Independence, for now, means preparing something manageable, not surrendering the stability you want to keep.

By June, you turn to the questions that would come before the design. Who is the page for? What should it accomplish? What content is already available? You write an intake questionnaire and try it against your existing practice brief. The exercise catches gaps that the tidy offer had concealed.

You revise the wording. An initial conversation should help define the work, not quietly become unlimited work itself. The offer grows clearer without growing larger.

Then you put it beyond the practice brief. You make a small round of local outreach, giving the service its first test near your home base in Veszprém. By the August review date, no paid commission has emerged.

You record that result. The difficult part is leaving it at its actual size: no paid work from this round, not a verdict on your ability. Perhaps the presentation needs sharpening. Perhaps the offer needs more exposure. Perhaps your examples need to show more. The test has not told you which.

At the six-month review in October, your office employment remains in place, and independent income remains unestablished. You outline two possible uses for the same limited evening time. Neither requires spending your savings now or leaving your home base.

You could present the clearer offer locally again and track responses, testing demand sooner while leaving less room for deeper study. Or you could pause new outreach for targeted accessibility study and one demonstration page, strengthening an example while postponing the question of whether anyone nearby will pay.

The next four evening hours cannot fully serve both purposes. Which next step feels worth that time while you preserve stability?

## 3. What One Page Can Tell You

In November, you give your evenings a boundary: four hours a week, not an open-ended second working day. Your office work continues, and the design study fits around it. On the page, the questions become smaller and more useful. Is the contrast readable? Do the headings make the structure clear? Does a form label explain what belongs beneath it?

You begin to explain these choices without reaching for a title you have not earned. You can apply basic accessibility considerations to a layout; that does not make you an accessibility specialist. The distinction gives your work a firmer edge. You are learning what to check, and how to say precisely what you have checked.

By January, the demonstration for a fictional local service is finished. You look between the layout and the short case study beside it. The explanation makes visible what the page alone cannot: why a heading sits where it does, why a label needs to be descriptive, why readable contrast belongs in the design from the beginning.

It is one completed example, not a working website. It is not evidence of accessibility compliance. Those limits stay in the description, even though a broader claim might sound more impressive. You want creative work near your current home in Veszprém, but you also want the stability you have kept. A careful account of your abilities serves both aims better than an inflated one.

In March, you review the demonstration again. A small adjustment offers the familiar comfort of something you can control. You can imagine another evening disappearing into polish: a little more clarity here, a better balance there. The example now says more about your reasoning than your earlier work did. Yet no revision can answer whether someone nearby would pay for the service.

Outreach remains paused. Independent income remains unestablished. The page is useful, but it cannot speak for a potential customer, and it cannot show how the layout would behave once implemented.

At the April six-month review, those two uncertainties sit side by side. You could return to a bounded local demand test, using the example to describe a narrow layout service—not implementation or a compliance assessment. That would expose your offer to uncertain responses before you have implementation skills. Or you could keep outreach paused and explore turning this demonstration into a simple working page, delaying the question of demand while investigating a practical gap.

Either next step would retain your office job and your base in Veszprém, with up to four evening hours a week and no spending commitment. Neither secures a client, income, or a finished site. You have a clearer example now. Which uncertainty do you want to tackle next: whether people nearby want what you can offer, or whether you can carry the layout into a working page?
