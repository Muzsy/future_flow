### Two Doors, Eight Hours

It began in November with a few evening sessions of basic digital layout. None of it was paid. You remind yourself of that without bitterness, as a fact on the table beside the tea.

You imagine saying it aloud to a neighbour, someone who might simply say no. The second path is to stay with practice, writing two more fictional case studies within four hours a week and holding outreach back. The question of whether anyone would pay would stay unanswered for longer.

You think about what you actually want from this: more creative work, near the home you have no wish to leave, without giving up the stability that lets you breathe. Moving abroad was never an option you would accept, and evening study is a trade you are willing to make. Outside, a tram bell fades down the street and the radiator ticks as it cools.

You pull a sheet of paper toward you and write both options in two columns, then stop with the pen resting on the page. Each column looks reasonable. Each one asks you to give up something you value. The choice is still waiting for you at the table.

### The Threshold

You sit at your kitchen table in Veszprém on a Thursday evening, your laptop glowing in the dimness. Six months have passed since you opened design software for the first time outside office hours. Your fingers know the wireframing tools now. You've written case studies. You have a folder of practice work that shows real progression—not mastery, but something genuine.

Your office job remains stable. Four hundred thousand forints arrive each month. Your colleagues still expect you at the administrative desk tomorrow morning, and the day after that. This predictability has weight. It has kept you steady while you've built these skills in stolen evening hours, your energy depleting from fifty-five down to nearly nothing by nine o'clock most nights.

But something has shifted. Your practice work has stopped feeling like hobby sketches. The case study you finished last month—the one explaining your reasoning behind layout hierarchy and whitespace—surprised you with its clarity. When you read it aloud to yourself, you recognized a voice emerging. Not a professional's voice yet, but someone who understands what they're doing.

Now you face a choice that has been waiting patiently, like a door you've walked past a hundred times without quite reaching for the handle.

Option one: reach out locally. Find someone in Veszprém who needs a website redesigned or a small digital project completed. Test whether the skills you've practiced matter to anyone but yourself. Four hours a week carved from your evenings, your eight available hours split between work-seeking and further practice. No guarantee. No client waiting. Just the uncertainty of discovering whether demand exists, whether you can translate your case studies into actual paid work. It would be faster. It would reveal the gap between confidence and reality.

Option two: keep practicing. Two more fictional projects. Two more case studies written with the care you're learning to apply. Stay inside the smaller, safer circle of your own learning. Keep your energy for the office during the day, know exactly what your evenings will contain. Postpone the question of whether anyone will pay for this work. The answer will still be waiting in six months.

Your cursor blinks. You're holding your breath without realizing it. The apartment is quiet. Your savings sit untouched—you don't need to risk them yet. Your office job doesn't know you're thinking about leaving it. Everything is still possible because you haven't chosen.

The weight of both futures presses down equally. To reach out is to face rejection or worse: indifference. To wait is to remain safe while time moves forward anyway, and you can feel it moving. You're forty. This goal lives in the next three years, somehow. Six months have already passed.

You close the laptop without deciding. Tomorrow you'll have to. Tonight, you let the question stay open, suspended between the two lives you could step toward.
