# Personal Scenario: A Manageable Career Experiment

A fictional profile used to test personal Scenario generation. These are three genuine AI-generated chapters, reproduced without rewriting, with their exact decision options. This is a development sample, separately audited by Claude; it is not an Official Life or a published user story.

The starting profile: employed in support in Germany; EUR 1,800 monthly income and EUR 600 liquid funds; four available hours per week; existing writing skills; time with a partner to protect. The primary goal is practical data-analysis skills and a manageable routine. The public career Scenario contributes inspiration and a secondary career-exploration goal.

## Chapter 1: A Question Small Enough to Finish

2026-10-08 to 2027-04-08

The question fits into a single sentence: which categories account for the most requests? In November, you begin with a free spreadsheet exercise built from fictional support requests. It is close enough to your work to give the task a clear purpose, without pretending the sample says anything about your actual workplace.

You give it two short sessions a week. Leaving some available time unused is part of the experiment, not a failure to take it seriously. You want practical confidence, but you also want a routine that leaves room for your partner. The exercise has to fit that life rather than quietly become another working day.

By January, the untidy rows give you something concrete to work through. Inconsistent category names have produced a misleading total. Before you can trust the answer, you have to make the labels consistent, check the counts, and build a simple summary table.

The finished table is modest. What matters is that you can explain why it needed those steps. A total can look convincing while counting things badly; cleaning the data is not decoration added after the interesting work. It is what makes the conclusion worth considering. Your experience is still introductory, but you now have a completed example rather than only an intention to learn.

In March, you turn to the explanation. Your existing writing skill helps you draw a boundary between what the exercise shows and what you might be tempted to claim. You describe the finding and include the limits of the fictional sample. This is practice, not proof that you are ready for a different job.

That distinction leaves the larger question open without making the work meaningless. You have tried a small piece of analysis and explained it clearly. You have not had to stake your employment or your savings on whether a career change will fit. Nor does finishing one exercise make every spare evening available for the next.

By April, the next step is a choice about what kind of progress you want. You could deepen this spreadsheet project with a chart and a clearer explanation, spending up to two hours a week on familiar ground. You could try free beginner SQL exercises for four weeks, using up to three hours a week to test another tool without committing to a job change. Or you could keep one short weekly review and postpone new tools for a month.

None requires dipping into your EUR 600 reserve. The sharper cost is time: repetition offers confidence but less novelty; SQL offers breadth but takes more of the room you want to protect; slowing down preserves that room while delaying growth. Which next step would help you build practical confidence while leaving room for time with your partner?

### Decision

How would you like to continue building practical analysis skills while protecting a manageable routine?

1. **Deepen the spreadsheet project** — Plan up to two hours a week to add a chart and strengthen the written explanation using free tools. This favors confidence through repetition and leaves spare-time headroom, but introduces fewer new techniques.

2. **Try a small SQL learning experiment** — Plan up to three hours a week for free beginner SQL exercises, with a review after four weeks. This tests interest in another analysis tool, but brings more unfamiliar material and uses more of your limited spare time. It does not commit you to changing jobs.

3. **Consolidate at a lighter pace** — Plan one short weekly review of the completed exercise and postpone new tools for a month. This gives more room to your personal routine and time with your partner, at the cost of slower skill growth. Both goals remain active.

Selected for this fictional test: **Try a small SQL learning experiment**. The next chapter follows that explicit choice.

## Chapter 2: What the Numbers Can Tell You

2027-04-08 to 2027-10-08

The two totals should agree. In July, you return to the fictional support-request exercise and find that your SQL category count does not match the spreadsheet summary. Neither result, on its own, tells you where the problem lies.

You check the labels. One inconsistency explains the mismatch. Once it is resolved, you can compare the totals and document what you checked before writing the explanation. It is a small piece of analysis, still beginner-level, but it gives the exercise a purpose beyond reaching the end of a tutorial. Getting a result is one task; checking whether it deserves confidence is another.

The foothold came in May, when you finished the four-week SQL experiment you had chosen. Free exercises and a three-hour weekly cap kept the attempt bounded. Filtering rows and grouping counts became things you could use with reference examples. Unfamiliar queries still needed those examples, and completing the experiment did not make you ready for a career change. It did give you something concrete to build on alongside your spreadsheet work and writing.

By September, the question is not simply how much more you could learn. You have four spare hours a week, but having them does not mean every one must become study time. An unfinished query can remain unfinished without becoming a claim on the rest of your life. Time with your partner belongs inside the goal you are trying to protect, not outside it as whatever remains. That reflection does not settle a schedule or tell you what your partner thinks; it clarifies what you want your own plan to respect.

In October, the next step offers three different kinds of evidence. A six-week mini-project, limited to two hours weekly, could test whether you can answer a question before turning to examples. It could also leave you facing frustrating dead ends while the career-fit question waits.

Four weeks spent reviewing public Germany-based analysis roles, at ninety minutes a week, could help you compare recurring tasks with the work you have actually tried. That would mean less hands-on practice, and role descriptions could not tell you whether a particular workplace would suit you.

Or you could revisit your existing exercises for six weeks at one hour weekly, leaving more time unallocated while giving familiar skills a chance to settle. The cost would be slower expansion and another delay in exploring a different career.

Your support employment remains unchanged, and none of these paths requires spending your EUR 600 reserve. The choice is about what to test next: your independence, the distance between your experience and role requirements, or the sustainability of a lighter routine. Which kind of progress would build confidence without making time with your partner the price?

### Decision

Which manageable next step would best serve your analysis goal while protecting personal time?

1. **Build one more independent mini-project** — Plan a six-week fictional-data project using free tools, capped at two hours weekly. Try answering one question before consulting examples. This tests practical independence, but delays exploring actual role requirements and may involve frustrating dead ends.

2. **Compare your experience with role requirements** — Plan a four-week review of publicly available Germany-based analysis role descriptions, capped at ninety minutes weekly. Map recurring tasks against your small exercises without applying yet. This may clarify what to learn next, but offers less hands-on practice and cannot establish whether you would enjoy a particular workplace.

3. **Consolidate at a lighter pace** — Plan six weeks of revisiting existing exercises, capped at one hour weekly, with no new tool or spending commitment. This leaves more unallocated time and may strengthen recall, but broadens your skills less and postpones the career-fit question.

Selected for this fictional test: **Compare your experience with role requirements**. The next chapter follows that explicit choice.

## Chapter 3: What the Checklist Cannot Tell You

2027-10-08 to 2028-04-08

The recurring tasks are less mysterious by the end of your four-week review: check the data, make a useful summary, explain what the result means. In November, you finish comparing public Germany-based analysis role descriptions with your beginner exercises. Keeping the review within ninety minutes a week has given it an ending rather than letting it expand into every spare evening.

Some requirements overlap with things you have practised. Others remain questions. You have identified where to direct your learning, not established that you are ready for a different job. Your support work remains unchanged.

In January, one of those priorities becomes concrete. You return to a simple category summary using fictional support-request data and free tools. Before interpreting the result, you write down what needs checking: missing categories, inconsistent labels, totals that do not agree.

The checklist changes the order of the exercise. A summary is no longer something to explain immediately; first, it is something to examine. You work through the checks in short sessions, giving the small task more attention without turning it into a larger project. What you have added is modest but usable: a basic way to check the quality of data before building an answer on it.

By March, you are considering what that improvement cannot settle. Completing an exercise at your own pace is different from doing analysis under workplace deadlines. A role description can name responsibilities without showing how they feel from day to day. Neither the review nor the checklist tells you whether a career change would fit the life you want to keep.

Time with your partner is part of that question, not something to account for after the learning is finished. Four available hours a week are a ceiling, not an obligation. Progress that occupies every spare evening would miss part of your main goal, even if it produced more practice.

In April, the next choice is about which uncertainty deserves your attention. None of the options requires spending your EUR 600 reserve. You could try a four-week analysis without a walkthrough, capped at two hours weekly: a clearer test of independence, with more room for frustration. You could spend an hour a week for four weeks examining stated deadlines, collaboration, and responsibilities, learning more about possible fit while doing less technical practice. Or you could revisit familiar exercises for six weeks at forty-five minutes weekly, protecting more personal time while postponing a stronger test.

Each path leaves something unanswered. Do you most need to find out what you can do with less guidance, what analysis work might ask of you, or how steadily you can keep learning without letting it crowd out the rest of your life?

### Decision

What should your next manageable experiment help you learn?

1. **Try a small analysis without a walkthrough** — Plan a four-week exercise using fictional data and free tools, capped at two hours weekly. Choose a question, check the data, and write a short answer, consulting references when needed. This tests independence but may bring more frustration than guided practice; it does not commit you to a career change.

2. **Examine the working conditions, not just the tools** — Plan a four-week comparison of public Germany-based role descriptions, capped at one hour weekly, focusing on deadlines, collaboration, and stated responsibilities. This may sharpen questions about career fit while leaving more spare time, but it provides less technical practice and cannot reveal an employer's actual day-to-day conditions.

3. **Consolidate at a gentler pace** — Plan six weeks of revisiting existing exercises, capped at forty-five minutes weekly, with no new tool or project. This keeps practice small and leaves more room for personal time, at the cost of slower progress and postponing a stronger test of career fit.
