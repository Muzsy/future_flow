## Chapter 1 — The Time Around the Repair

## Chapter 3 — What Preparation Can Tell You

2027-10-07 to 2028-04-07

In November, you return to the workshop you have already chosen. The choice still stands, but it has not become a date on a calendar or a payment made. Its schedule and fee remain unconfirmed. For now, you consider what would make it worth attending: not encouragement to build something bigger, but help telling a manageable bicycle-maintenance service from an expensive second workload.

That distinction matters. You want to learn whether independent work can be useful and sustainable alongside your customer-support job. A smaller first attempt would count. A service that consumed your financial buffer or treated personal time as disposable would not. Choosing preparation first has kept paid appointments deferred; it has also left the central question unanswered.

By February, your existing costing worksheet and trial log give that question sharper edges. You prepare a short list for the workshop: How should a minimum charge account for work beyond the repair itself? Where does unpaid administration belong in the calculation? What happens when someone cancels? What requirements would you need to verify before offering the service at all?

The questions make the proposed one-appointment weekly pilot easier to examine. Repair time is only one part of an appointment; intake, messages, costs, and handover also need room. Your earlier rehearsal and tracking tools help you see those pieces without mistaking practice for paid demand.

You add a separate checklist for the workshop itself: timing, total fee, and relevance to that small pilot. It is a concrete piece of preparation you did not have before. It is not proof that you have attended, found customers, or earned anything.

On April 7, the useful questions are still there, and so are the limits of what they can answer. Your maintenance kit remains yours. Your regular job remains in place. The USD 3,350 recorded in liquid funds is still a buffer, not money already committed to the business.

Now the next step asks something different of you. You could resolve the workshop details first, keeping the preparation-first approach while waiting longer to learn about demand. You could return to preparing a paid pilot capped at one appointment a week, checking requirements and competence limits before offering bookings, using what you already have without new spending or borrowing. Or you could deliberately pause preparation and outreach for three months, protecting personal time while accepting slower learning.

None of these paths requires abandoning the goal. Each leaves something important waiting: practical guidance, a test of usefulness, or space outside work. Which kind of waiting are you willing to carry next?
