## Chapter 3 — Room for What Matters

2027-10-07 to 2028-04-07

In November, you give the search a stopping point—not a destination, but a question precise enough to answer. Could housing and travel costs in another city leave enough money and time for the friendship you want to keep?

That changes the shape of your remote exploration. You are no longer asking it to reveal an entire future. You are asking whether a possible move could make room for both a better-fitting daily life and an important connection. A place would not fit simply because you could afford to live there if maintaining the friendship became something you could rarely afford to do.

No city has yet emerged as the destination. No housing option has been established as affordable, and no travel arrangement is in place. The short exploratory visit remains open. Your earlier choice to keep exploring remotely has limited the financial commitment, but it has also left the feeling of another city beyond reach.

By February, another boundary becomes clear: you can leave space in your own life without filling in someone else’s.

Your long-standing friend’s preferences and availability remain unknown. You can allow for contact and possible visits; you cannot turn those allowances into a shared schedule. With seven available hours each week, the distinction matters. An exploration that occupies every spare hour would leave little room for the connection it is meant to preserve. The empty space is part of the plan, not a failure to finish it.

In April, you are still in your current home, with your employment unchanged and USD 4,800 in liquid funds. The remote approach has protected that money. It has not shown you how an ordinary day elsewhere would feel.

The next step can be smaller than a verdict on moving. A visit with a firm USD 600 ceiling and no borrowing could seek firsthand evidence, though you would still need to choose a destination and verify arrangements before booking. Another focused remote comparison could address housing and travel affordability without spending now, while leaving everyday fit untested. Or a four-week experiment at home could explore whether a different rhythm helps, reserving your own time for local exploration and offering contact without assuming your friend’s response.

Each test leaves something unanswered. You want movement without unmanageable debt, and change without treating a close relationship as expendable. Which uncertainty will you test next: the feel of another city, its affordability, or the possibilities within your current routine?
