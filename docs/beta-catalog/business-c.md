## Chapter 1 — The Work Around the Repair

2026-10-07 to 2027-04-07

The useful part of the January workshop is not about repairing bicycles. It is about everything that happens around a repair: the messages before it, the supplies needed for it, the record kept afterward. A brief task can occupy much more than the time your hands spend doing it.

You pay USD 80 to learn that distinction, and leave with a basic way to estimate a whole job. It gives your idea a sharper outline, though not an answer. Knowing how to count the work does not tell you whether anyone will pay enough for it.

Back in November, you had deliberately made the question smaller. You were not planning a second full-time job. You wanted to find out whether a narrow bicycle-maintenance service could be useful and sustainable alongside your customer-support work. Six available hours a week had to contain the whole experiment, not just its most satisfying parts. Borrowing money or letting the project crowd out close relationships would defeat the purpose. You had not advertised or taken bookings.

By March, that smaller ambition takes the form of a one-page intake checklist. On it, you separate tasks within your present maintenance skills from work you would decline. The boundary matters as much as the offer. You also outline a log for time and expenses, carrying the workshop’s lesson into something you could use: a way to see what a job costs rather than remembering only the repair itself.

The page makes the idea more concrete. It also makes its limits plain. Nothing on it proves demand. No customer trial has happened. You can describe the experiment more clearly now, but you still cannot say whether its pace or earnings would justify the effort.

In April, six months into the idea, the next step has a price attached. A modest dedicated kit would cost USD 320, reducing your liquid funds from USD 3,520 to USD 3,200. It could support preparation for a trial capped at two small jobs a month, provided readiness checks are satisfied and the tasks stay within your skills. Buying it would not produce customers, bookings or earnings. It would put some money behind the question.

Keeping the cash offers a different kind of progress: tighter task boundaries, fuller cost estimates, and a clearer account of what equipment a trial actually needs. That protects your buffer, but leaves the central uncertainty untouched for longer.

You want independent work to be useful, not merely well prepared. You also want the experiment to fit the life it is meant to improve. The checklist cannot choose between those aims for you. Do you spend on readiness now, or preserve the cash and refine the service before testing it?

## Chapter 2 — What the Checklist Cannot Tell You

You work through hypothetical jobs, accounting for the less visible tasks that could make a short repair consume a much longer stretch of your week.

## Chapter 3 — What the Blank Record Can’t Tell You

2027-10-07 to 2028-04-07

In November, you rehearse everything around the repair except the repair itself. You set out the maintenance kit, check the tools, work through cleanup and consider what needs recording. The equipment you chose to buy makes the checklist tangible: each item now belongs to a sequence you can practise rather than merely describe.

It is a modest kind of progress, but a real one. You are learning to organize the beginning and end of a job. You are not demonstrating new repair competence, and there is no customer waiting whose booking might reveal how long the whole process takes. The distinction matters. A smooth rehearsal cannot answer every question you bought the kit to explore.

In January, the trial record gives those unanswered questions somewhere to go. You separate inquiries from requests beyond your scope, and time spent from direct costs. The administrative work has a place beside the maintenance work; neither gets to disappear from the accounting simply because one feels more like running a bicycle service.

You also write down reasons to stop. A request beyond your skills. Work that spills past the six hours a week available alongside your regular job. Pressure to borrow. These are not signs that the experiment has failed before it begins. They are boundaries that could keep a small attempt from becoming something you never meant to undertake.

Then there is the blank space beneath the headings. No bookings, no customer earnings, no evidence of demand. You have made a way to measure usefulness, but nothing to measure yet.

By March, the question has shifted. You know more about arranging the work. You still do not know whether anyone wants this limited service, or whether delivering it would fit into your life. Another revision of the same documents could make them clearer without bringing either answer closer.

### Decision

What kind of next step would best test the bicycle-service goal without letting it overtake the rest of your life?

- **Test interest without taking bookings** — Make the next phase an interest-only test of the narrow service description, with no purchases or customer commitments. This preserves cash and limits delivery pressure, but expressions of interest would not prove willingness to pay or practical sustainability.
- **Work toward a one-job trial** — Choose a trial plan capped at one customer job at a time, using the existing kit. Before offering a booking, check applicable requirements, a suitable workspace and the limits of your competence. This aims for more concrete evidence, but takes more preparation and responsibility; selecting it does not establish that those checks pass or that a customer appears.
