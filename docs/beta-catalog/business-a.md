## Chapter 1 — Counting the Whole Job

2026-10-07 to 2027-04-07

At the introductory workshop in November, the useful exercise asks you to count more than the time spent turning a wrench. Arranging a job belongs in the calculation. So does explaining what you have done. A repair that looks brief in isolation might occupy considerably more of your week once you include everything around it.

The USD 80 buys you a basic costing method, not proof that a bicycle-repair service will work. Still, it changes the question. Instead of asking only whether you can do a task, you can begin asking what the whole job would require—and whether the price could justify that effort.

In January, preparation takes a more tangible form: a small kit of bicycle-maintenance tools, purchased for USD 220. Alongside it, you write a proposed service scope. Basic maintenance within your present skills belongs inside the boundary; unfamiliar or safety-critical work stays outside it. The exclusions matter as much as the offer. They keep a small experiment from becoming a claim you cannot support.

You have not rented premises, taken on debt or left your customer-support job. The toolkit makes the possibility more concrete without turning it into a working business.

By March, the workshop’s lesson has become the difficult part of imagining a first customer. Your six available hours each week would have to contain messages, preparation and cleanup as well as maintenance. Your experience explaining things in customer support could help, but clear explanations cannot establish what people will pay. Nor do you want every spare hour to become business time, with close relationships left to fit around it.

In April, the two purchases total USD 300, and USD 3,300 remains in liquid funds. There are still no customers or repair earnings to weigh against that spending. What you have is a narrower offer, a way to count its costs and a choice about which uncertainty to tackle next.

A portable repair stand would cost another USD 180. With it, you could adopt a conditional plan for at most one basic-maintenance appointment a week, checking applicable requirements and a suitable workspace before offering work. That would bring you closer to testing the practical job, though it would not supply customers or guarantee worthwhile returns.

Alternatively, you could keep the toolkit and your funds as they are, draft a short service description and seek nonbinding feedback on needs and pricing. That would preserve flexibility, but delay learning what paid work actually demands. Interest would still not be a booking.

You want to learn whether independent work can be useful and sustainable—not merely whether you can keep preparing for it. Which uncertainty should your next small step address: readiness to attempt the work, or demand before spending more?

### Decision

Which uncertainty should your next small step address?

- **Equip for a tightly bounded pilot** — Buy a portable repair stand for USD 180 and adopt a plan to attempt at most one basic-maintenance appointment per week, only after checking applicable requirements and a suitable workspace. This puts more money into readiness and could produce practical evidence, but finding customers and making the work worthwhile remain uncertain. Keep the regular job and protect time outside the experiment.
- **Validate demand before spending more** — Keep the existing toolkit and liquid funds. Adopt a research-first plan: draft a short service description and seek nonbinding feedback about needs and pricing before offering repairs. This limits financial exposure and leaves more flexibility for relationships, but delays hands-on evidence about paid work. Interest, if any emerges, would not count as a booking.

## Chapter 2 — What the Tools Cannot Tell You

2027-04-07 to 2027-10-07

In May, you turn the conditional pilot plan into a checklist. A suitable workspace. Applicable requirements. A narrow service scope. A way to count the time for the whole job, not just the repair itself.

The portable stand is yours, alongside the compact toolkit, but neither answers where paid work can happen. That distinction gives the checklist its purpose. You have already chosen a small beginning—no more than one basic-maintenance appointment a week—and now you make the conditions for that beginning explicit. Until workspace and requirements are settled, you do not offer appointments.

In July, you rehearse the sequence without a customer's bicycle. Setup comes first, then intake, then explaining the limits of the service, and finally cleanup. The repair is no longer the only part you have to account for. Each surrounding step belongs in the costing too, even though none is the work you originally pictured when you imagined a bicycle-repair service.

You finish with a clearer grasp of how to plan and time that workflow. It is real preparation, but it is not a customer test. It cannot tell you whether anyone needs this particular service or what they would pay for it.

By September, the distinction matters more. Another tool would be something tangible to acquire, yet it would leave the central questions untouched. With six available hours a week, investigating those questions would take time away from practice. A smaller first attempt still fits your goal; endless preparation would not answer it.

October brings you to a choice about what kind of uncertainty deserves attention next. Your regular job remains in place, and the experiment has produced no appointments or earnings.

A limited readiness review would cost USD 120, leaving USD 3,000 in liquid funds. It could clarify workspace needs and applicable requirements, but paying for it would not secure permission or a place to work. A no-spend demand-research plan would instead reserve up to two hours a week to explore what potential customers need, without offering repairs or treating interest as bookings. Or you could pause preparation and spending for three months, keeping the equipment and the longer-term goal while protecting time outside your job.

You want to learn whether independent work can be useful and sustainable—not merely become better prepared to imagine it. Which comes next: investigating whether you can offer the service, exploring whether people might want it, or allowing the experiment to wait?

### Decision

Which uncertainty should guide the next stage of your bicycle-service experiment?

- **Commission a limited readiness review** — Spend USD 120 to commission a review of workspace needs and applicable requirements. This prioritizes whether a bounded pilot is practical, while leaving USD 3,000 in liquid funds. The review may identify further obstacles; purchasing it does not establish approval, a workspace or permission to offer repairs.
- **Explore demand without offering repairs** — Choose a no-spend research plan focused on what potential customers need and what they consider worth paying for. Limit it to two hours a week within your existing available time. This protects cash and may clarify demand, but conversations are not bookings and the workspace conditions remain unresolved.
- **Pause deliberately for three months** — Pause business preparation and spending for three months, retaining your equipment and the longer-term goal. This protects time outside your regular job and avoids spending merely to maintain momentum, but delays learning whether independent work could be useful and sustainable.

## Chapter 3 — Room for an Unhelpful Answer

2027-10-07 to 2028-04-07

The review gives you questions, not a green light.

When it arrives in November, the distinction matters. You paid for a limited look at workspace and requirements, and the result is a preliminary checklist: assess a separate workspace rather than assume your current home will do; check the requirements against the basic maintenance you actually intend to offer. Your compact toolkit and portable repair stand remain useful equipment, but owning them does not make a place suitable for operating.

You have a clearer sequence now. You still have no approved workspace, appointments or repair income. The regular job stays in place, and even a future pilot remains conditional, limited to one appointment a week. The review makes that smaller first attempt more concrete without making it ready.

In January, the work shifts to the wording of a question.

You practice drafting a short interview about maintenance needs. Before explaining your proposed service, you ask what someone has needed recently, what alternatives they already use and what makes getting maintenance inconvenient. The order leaves room for an answer that does not help your idea. Perhaps the problem you hope to solve is not the problem worth solving; the questions need to allow that possibility.

Beside the draft, your worksheet separates expressions of interest from actual paid demand. There are no customer answers to enter yet. Still, you have improved something important: how to seek evidence without turning every polite response into encouragement.

By April, the checklist and worksheet point in different directions. One asks whether a workable setup can be arranged. The other asks whether people would value the service at all. More equipment would answer neither.

With six hours a week available alongside your job, your next step has to earn its place. Four weeks of discovery, capped at two hours a week, could explore needs without offering repairs or taking bookings. A USD 100 follow-up workspace search could investigate practical conditions, leaving USD 2,900 in your buffer—but would secure neither permission nor paying customers. An eight-week pause could protect money and leave more room outside the project, while postponing the learning you started this experiment to gain.

You want independent work to be useful and sustainable, not merely possible on paper. Which uncertainty deserves your next bounded step—and is this the time to take it?

### Decision

Which uncertainty should your next bounded step address?

- **Test interest before spending more** — Adopt a four-week customer-discovery plan capped at two hours per week, seeking conversations about maintenance needs without offering repairs or accepting bookings. This preserves your cash buffer and could reveal whether the idea deserves further effort, but expressions of interest would not prove paid demand or resolve workspace requirements.
- **Investigate a workable location** — Spend USD 100 to commission a targeted follow-up search for candidate external workspaces and their stated conditions for a small maintenance pilot. This leaves USD 2,900 in liquid funds and prioritizes practical feasibility. It does not secure a space, establish permission or show that customers will pay.
- **Pause without abandoning the goal** — Adopt an eight-week pause on business spending and outreach, then reassess whether the experiment still merits your limited time. Keep the equipment and regular job. This protects cash and leaves more room outside the project, but delays learning whether independent work could be useful or sustainable.

This final choice has not been made.
